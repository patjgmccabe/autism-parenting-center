IEP vs 504 Plan: What's the Difference?

September 18, 2026  ·  9 min read

← Back to Blog

If you're weighing IEP vs 504 for your child, here's the short version. An IEP (Individualized Education Program) is for kids who need specially designed instruction, meaning changes to what or how they're taught, plus services like speech or occupational therapy. It comes from a federal law called the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). A 504 plan is for kids who can learn the regular curriculum but need accommodations to get to it, like extra time on tests, a quiet space, or movement breaks. It comes from Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, a civil rights law.

IEPs come with stricter rules: a written plan, measurable goals, a required team, yearly reviews, and strong parent rights. 504 plans are more flexible and cover more kids, but they have fewer built-in protections. Many autistic children qualify for an IEP because autism is one of IDEA's named disability categories. A diagnosis alone doesn't guarantee one, though. A child who's keeping up academically but struggles with sensory overload may get a 504 plan instead.

Key takeaways

  • An IEP changes what or how your child is taught. A 504 plan changes how your child accesses the same teaching.
  • IEPs come from IDEA (a special education law). 504 plans come from Section 504 (a civil rights law).
  • Autism is one of IDEA's 13 disability categories, but your child must also need special education to qualify for an IEP.
  • IEPs have measurable goals, a required team, annual reviews, and stronger legal protections for parents.
  • A child with an IEP doesn't need a separate 504 plan. The IEP can include every accommodation a 504 would.
  • You can request an evaluation for either one in writing at any time.

What is an IEP?

An IEP (Individualized Education Program) is a legally binding written plan, created under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, that sets out the special education instruction, related services, and measurable goals a public school must provide to an eligible child with a disability.

In plain English, an IEP is the school's written promise about how it will teach your child differently. It lists where your child is right now (the "present levels"), what they'll work toward this year (the goals), and exactly what the school will do to get them there. That can mean minutes per week with a speech therapist, time in a smaller classroom, a one-on-one aide, social skills instruction, or a modified curriculum.

The IEP is written by a team, and you're a required member of it. So are a general education teacher, a special education teacher, and a school district representative. Your child can join too when it makes sense, and must be invited once transition planning starts in the teen years. For a fuller walkthrough of the document and the meeting, see our guide on what an IEP is and what to ask for.

The key word is instruction. An IEP isn't just about making school easier to access. It's about changing the teaching itself.

What is a 504 plan?

A 504 plan is a written plan, created under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, that lists the accommodations a school will provide so a student with a disability has equal access to education alongside their peers.

Section 504 is a civil rights law. It says schools that receive federal money can't discriminate against students with disabilities. A 504 plan is how a school puts that into practice for one child.

The focus is access, not a different curriculum. Your child learns the same material as everyone else, and the plan removes barriers that get in the way. For an autistic child, common 504 accommodations include noise-canceling headphones, a quiet place to take tests, preferential seating away from the hallway door, advance warning before schedule changes, visual schedules, breaks when overwhelmed, and extra time on assignments.

504 plans are less formal than IEPs. Federal law doesn't require specific goals or a set team, though most schools use a written plan and hold a meeting with parents and staff. That flexibility can be helpful, but it also means you'll want to check that the plan is specific enough to actually be followed in the classroom.

IEP vs 504: What's the real difference for autistic kids?

The simplest way to explain it: a 504 plan changes the environment, and an IEP changes the education.

Picture a child who reads at grade level but melts down in a loud cafeteria. A 504 plan might let him eat in a quieter room. Now picture a child who struggles to understand what classmates mean, can't yet hold a back-and-forth conversation, and is falling behind in writing. Accommodations alone won't fix that. He needs someone actually teaching him those skills, which is what an IEP does.

Many autistic kids fall somewhere in between, and that's where the decision gets hard. Some start with a 504 and move to an IEP as demands grow in later grades. Others get an IEP early and keep it for years. The turning point for most families is the moment it becomes clear that accommodations alone aren't enough. That's usually the clearest sign an IEP evaluation is worth requesting.

Who qualifies for an IEP?

To qualify for an IEP, a child has to pass a two-part test under IDEA. First, they need to have one of 13 disability categories, and autism is one of them. Second, that disability has to affect their education enough that they need special education to make progress.

That second part trips up a lot of families. A school may say, "He has autism, but his grades are fine, so he doesn't qualify." Educational performance isn't only about grades, though. Communication, social skills, behavior, and daily functioning at school can all count, and many states spell this out. If your child is struggling in those areas, say so clearly and in writing.

IEPs are common. In the 2022–23 school year, 7.5 million students ages 3 to 21 received services under IDEA, about 15 percent of all public school students, according to the National Center for Education Statistics. Autism is a meaningful part of that picture: autism accounted for 16 percent of male students served under IDEA, compared with 7 percent of female students.

Who qualifies for a 504 plan?

A 504 plan has a broader bar. A child qualifies if they have a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities. That list includes learning, reading, concentrating, thinking, communicating, and even eating and sleeping.

Unlike an IEP, your child doesn't need to require special education to qualify. They just need a disability that gets in the way of equal access to school. That's why 504 plans often cover kids with ADHD, anxiety, diabetes, food allergies, or autistic kids who are doing well academically but need support around sensory, social, or routine challenges.

Fewer students have 504-only plans than IEPs. In 2021–22, students with disabilities served only under Section 504 made up about 4 percent of total K–12 enrollment, according to the U.S. Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights.

One thing to watch: a 504 plan shouldn't be offered as a "consolation prize" when a child actually needs an IEP. If your child needs direct teaching of skills, a 504 isn't the right tool.

How do an IEP and a 504 plan compare side by side?

Here's the difference between an IEP and a 504 plan at a glance:

  IEP 504 plan
Legal basis Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), a special education law Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, a civil rights law
Who qualifies Child has one of 13 disability categories (including autism) and needs special education to make progress Child has an impairment that substantially limits a major life activity, such as learning or communicating
What it provides Specially designed instruction, related services (speech, OT, counseling), measurable goals, plus accommodations Accommodations and supports so the child can access regular education
Written plan required? Yes, with specific required parts Not required by federal law, but most schools write one
Where it applies Public schools, ages 3 through 21 or high school graduation Any program receiving federal funds, including many private schools and colleges
How to request one Written request for a special education evaluation Written request for a Section 504 evaluation
How often it's reviewed At least once a year, with a full reevaluation at least every 3 years Periodically (many districts review yearly), and before any major change in placement
Parent protections Strong: written notice, consent, independent evaluations, due process Fewer: notice, records access, impartial hearing, complaints to the Office for Civil Rights

How do I request an IEP or a 504 plan?

For either one, the first step is the same: put your request in writing. Send a dated letter or email to your school's principal and special education coordinator asking for an evaluation, and say which one you're requesting. Briefly describe what you're seeing at home and school, and attach any outside reports, like an autism diagnosis or a therapist's notes.

For an IEP, once you give written consent, federal law gives the school 60 days to complete the evaluation, unless your state sets its own timeline. Then the team meets to decide eligibility and, if your child qualifies, writes the IEP. For a 504, there's no federal deadline, but the school must act within a reasonable time.

Keep copies of everything. Emails beat phone calls because they create a record.

Can my child have both an IEP and a 504 plan?

In practice, no, and you don't need both. An IEP can include every accommodation a 504 plan would, like extra time, sensory breaks, or preferential seating. So if your child has an IEP, those supports simply go into the IEP.

Your child still keeps their Section 504 rights, though. Every student with an IEP is also protected from disability discrimination under Section 504. The IEP is just the document that holds everything.

Where families do see a shift is over time. A child might have a 504 plan in first grade, then need an IEP in fourth grade when schoolwork gets more demanding. Or a teen who has made great progress might move from an IEP to a 504 plan in high school. Neither move is a failure. It's the plan catching up to where your child is right now.

If the school suggests dropping your child's IEP, ask what data supports that decision, and remember you can disagree in writing.

Which one is right for my autistic child?

Ask yourself one question: Does my child need to be taught differently, or just supported differently?

If your child needs direct help building skills, such as communication, social understanding, reading, writing, or managing emotions, push for an IEP evaluation. If your child is keeping up with the work but gets tripped up by noise, transitions, or the social chaos of a school day, a 504 plan may be enough.

Autism is common enough that most schools have seen your situation many times. The CDC estimates that about 1 in 31 8-year-olds in its monitoring network sites were identified with autism in 2022, according to the CDC's ADDM Network.

Still, you know your child best. Trust what you see, bring it to the table, and don't be afraid to ask for an evaluation even if the school thinks things are "fine."

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference in IEP vs 504?

An IEP provides special education, meaning specially designed instruction, services like speech therapy, and measurable goals, under IDEA. A 504 plan provides accommodations so a child can access the regular curriculum, under a civil rights law. The short version: an IEP changes how your child is taught, and a 504 plan changes how your child accesses what's taught.

Does an autism diagnosis automatically qualify my child for an IEP?

No. Autism is one of IDEA's 13 disability categories, but your child must also need special education to make progress. Schools should look at communication, social skills, and behavior, not only grades. If your child doesn't qualify for an IEP, they may still qualify for a 504 plan.

Is an IEP better than a 504 plan?

Neither is better in general. An IEP offers more services and stronger legal protections, so it's the right fit when a child needs skills taught directly. A 504 plan is simpler and works well for kids who can keep up with the regular curriculum once barriers are removed. The right plan is the one that matches your child's needs.

Can a school deny an IEP and offer a 504 plan instead?

Yes, if the evaluation team decides your child doesn't meet IDEA's criteria. If you disagree, you can request an independent educational evaluation, ask for mediation, or file for a due process hearing. Put your disagreement in writing and ask the school to explain its decision and the data behind it.

Do IEPs and 504 plans follow my child to college?

IEPs end when a student graduates high school with a regular diploma or ages out of IDEA. Colleges don't use IEPs, but they must provide reasonable accommodations under Section 504 and the ADA. Your child will need to contact the college's disability services office, share documentation, and request accommodations themselves.

Can I ask to switch my child from a 504 plan to an IEP?

Yes. Send a written request for a special education evaluation and describe why the current 504 plan isn't enough, with examples. Once you consent, the school must evaluate within the federal 60-day timeline or your state's timeline. Many autistic kids move to an IEP as schoolwork and social demands increase.
